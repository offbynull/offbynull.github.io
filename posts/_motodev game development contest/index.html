Things I've tried: Chasing the Indie Gamedev Dream

MONTH DAY, YEAR

In 2007, at the age of 22, I resigned from what was arguably my first real software engineering job to enter an indie gamedev competition. That competition was Motorola's MOTODEV Game Developer Challenge, which asked developers to create new games for the then-flagship Motorola KRZR K1.

My entry to the competition was Mad Kaz, a vertical platformer backed by a custom game engine. This post recounts the chain of misguided decisions that ultimately led to Mad Kaz and the career aftermath that followed, pieced together from old memories and archival records.

Entering the competition

While the details on how I came across the competition are lost, the motivations behind entering aren't. In late 2006, I was roughly a year into working as a software engineer on a decently popular mobile social networking app. My enthusiasm for the work wasn't as strong as it was when I had originally been hired, and so I quietly decided to start looking for what would come next.

Somewhere between looking through job openings and weighing going back for another round of college, I came across the MOTODEV Game Developer Challenge. I figured that if I could make a game that was impressive enough, it would serve as a launchpad into something greater: starting an indie game studio, or a gig working on console games, or maybe just gaining credibility in the broader online gamedev community. I had the relevant experience. I had grown up playing PC games in the 1990s and early 2000s, and the low-level mobile development experience I had gained on-the-job was applicable to developing games. I had written graphics code, networking code, low-level optimizations, math-heavy code, and code that closely resembled game loops.

The MOTODEV Game Developer Challenge opened to contestants on February 21, 2007, running until August 10, 2007. I submitted my two-week resignation notice to my employer around mid-March, keeping the reason behind the resignation quiet except for a few close friends and coworkers. I spent those last two weeks prepping for the competition by registering an LLC and having a high school acquaintance put its website together.

Building the engine: The MMO that never was

Early April, once my employment officially ended, I began work on the actual game. The idea I came up with was a top-down 2D MMO virtual world, similar to Habbo Hotel but for mobile phones. I had pitched a similar idea to an executive at my employer, at a reward lunch he had taken me to for fixing a blocker bug. He wasn't in love with the idea. An MMO would be costly to produce and the market for it would be tiny. Mobile phones around that time were severely constrained and mobile Internet connectivity was expensive. Only people with high-end phones and mobile Internet would be able to play.

From my standpoint, those drawbacks didn't matter all that much. I was aiming to make something impressive for the competition, not aiming to make a commercially viable mass-market product. At the time, I had around $7,500 to my name and was still living in my parents' home, in the same bedroom I grew up in. Before I could do anything, I needed a KRZR K1 or similar device. I went to a local store selling imported mobile phones and picked up the closest device in stock to the KRZR K1, the Motorola SLVR L7. Alongside that I purchased a Samsung SGH-D520, just so I could have another device to try playing the game on.

With the devices in hand, the next step was to prep my bedroom as a work area. With only 3 to 4 months left to deliver, I needed to minimize distractions. I took my mother's thick faux-fur blanket and duct taped its edges to the walls bordering my 108sqft bedroom's only window, effectively blacking out the room. By eliminating outside light and keeping the room's lights switched off, the only light source remaining would be from the monitor directly in front of me.

I started out by making a plan. I charted out tasks and timelines in a Gantt chart, mocked out a UI flow, and mocked out a demonstration map. The idea was for the MMO to be more of a platform than a game. Scripts and assets would flow directly from the Internet, driving the game's logic and gameplay. The ultimate goal was to have a massive explorable world where users themselves would expand the world with their own content.

Every task in that Gantt chart was doable, but not by a single developer in three or four months. The timelines, which were already wishful thinking to begin with, showed that development would stretch well past the competition's deadline of early August. And so, the MMO idea was a non-starter.

What wasn't a non-starter were the components in that timeline. Many of them, I figured, would be needed regardless of the game idea I eventually settled on. And so, I started building them. First, a font renderer. One week, gone. Then, a Huffman compressor. Another two to three days, gone. Then, a virtual machine and assembler. Another twenty-one days, gone. Then, image transformations and real-time audio mixing. Another three to five days, gone.

By the time I was done, I had spent roughly a third of my available time speculating on engine features for an unknown game. I had figured that any real game would need this base set of features. It would need to display text in more than just the phone's default font. It would need to support Internet multiplayer: Mobile Internet was billed by the megabyte; multiplayer traffic needed compression. It would need to support user-generated content: Extendible game logic required a virtual machine, like QuakeC did for Quake, while image and audio transformations would make user-supplied assets flexible.

By mid-May, with a month and a half already gone, I finally settled on a game idea.

Cutting scope: Choosing Mad Kaz

I decided that the game I wanted to build would be a re-imagining of NS-SHAFT and NS-TOWER blended into one. NS-SHAFT and NS-TOWER were introduced to me by a college classmate as a way of killing time while we were held up in the computer labs. The twist with this re-imagining was that it'd be a narrative experience with levels, where each level would be unique in terms of graphics, audio, and gameplay mechanics.

I came up with the set of levels that I wanted the game to have. The title character, Mad Kaz, would climb up from inside the Earth and eventually out into space, before encountering a boss battle and falling back down to Earth: Original level plan document. I named it Mad Kaz - The Beginning.

Shortly after, I began looking for contract artists to help piece it all together. For music, I found a Norwegian chiptunes musician by the name of Xonic the Fox. Xonic produced 3 MIDI tracks based on a small set of requirements describing the mood each track should evoke. For concept art, I reached out to a high school classmate, William, to create a few basic sketches. There were no requirements. I simply asked him to envision how things should look. For pixel art, I found Mykola, a Ukrainian pixel artist. Mykola agreed to help reimagine the concept art as pixel art.

Cutting scope, again: Spread too thin

Even with a plan and the extra help, it turned out that it was all still too much. The roughly 3 remaining months weren't enough time to both complete the game's engine and build out that much game logic and art. Pixel art didn't start flowing in until mid-June. I hadn't begun coding up the majority of the game's levels, outside of a few practice levels, until mid-July. Before that, I was still primarily trying to put the pieces of the game's engine together.

Old emails from Mykola show that I wasn't keeping it together. Keeping myself confined to a blacked out room wasn't helping. With no outside light seeping in, it was impossible to tell if it was day or night. I would sleep and wake at random times, sometimes opening the bedroom door to use the kitchen or washroom thinking it was daytime only to realize it was midnight.

I decided to cut scope again. I scrapped the original level design and instead restructured the game into zones. Each zone would have multiple levels that re-used assets and had similar game logic, similar to the original Sonic The Hedgehog games.

The original plan was to have 12 unique levels. The new plan was to have 6 unique zones: Intro, Homework, Hippie, Time Trials, Giagantor, and Author. The opening and closing zones, Intro and Author, were write-offs. Author Zone was nothing more than a single level that acted as a credit sequence, paying homage to friends and those who worked on the game. Intro Zone was a tutorial, getting the player familiar with gameplay mechanics. The 4 zones in between were the real content: Zone breakdown and analysis.

Of those 4 zones, Homework most resembled a real narrative experience. It had a story arc somewhat similar to Mad Kaz's original level plan. There was cityscape climbing, racing downward to outrun a bomb, and there was even a boss fight. To avoid delays, I had pieced together the art for it myself instead of relying on my pixel artist, scribbling out schoolbook-doodle style graphics in Microsoft Paint: Graph paper, pencil drawings, stick men, etc.

The remaining 3 zones, Hippie, Time Trials, and Giagantor, were added afterwards by gluing together various pixel art pieces into something that bordered on being passable. Each of the 3 had a niche. Hippie dealt with changing perception. Time Trials was a race against time. Giagantor played with size and shake mechanics.

July 26th was when the last zone was completed. Bugfixes and tweaks ran through July 27th and 28th. Even though there were still roughly 2 weeks remaining until the competition deadline, I was spent. I never sat through a full playthrough of the entire game, or even a full playthrough of certain zones within the game. I could have balanced the gameplay to make it fun to play (it wasn't). I could have fixed typos. I could have finished implementing the game engine's networking component. The list goes on.

And so, in the end, Mad Kaz wasn't so much a game as much as it was a tech demo for a game engine. The 4 months I had spent putting things together weren't enough for me to build out both a game engine and the game's originally intended levels. The result was a mishmash of unbalanced and narratively disjointed levels that tried to shove in as many engine features as possible.

Submission: The anticlimax

I submitted Mad Kaz into the competition on July 29th, 2007, about 2 weeks before the August 10th deadline. A confirmation email arrived the next day, and that was it. I never heard back from the people running the competition about their thoughts on Mad Kaz or how it ranked against other games in the competition. I showed the game to a few real-life friends. They thought it was alright. I had an online friend load it up on his phone and give it a try. He thought it was alright. I posted some screenshots in an IRC chatroom. Someone replied saying the graphics were bad. I wrote my father letting him know what I had been up to after 4 months unemployed. His response was that I would always be a winner to him. And, that was it.

Looking back, there was an expectation that submitting Mad Kaz would result in some kind of defining moment of pride and accomplishment. Instead, it was dejection and regret. I had spent four months working in darkness, watching my savings dwindle, only to end up with something that was nowhere near what I originally intended.

Aftermath: Back to reality

Around that time, I was using a spreadsheet to keep track of my checking and savings balance. Money was beginning to run tight. The records show that I started the competition with around $7,500 to my name. By the time I submitted Mad Kaz in late July, I had around $1,500. A month later, it went down to $1,100. A month after that, it went further down to $600.

In early August, shortly after submitting Mad Kaz, I began looking for work again. I sent applications to whatever places were looking to hire, but I was also trying to leverage my work on Mad Kaz to get hired at a game studio. I decided to open-source Mad Kaz so potential interviewers could review it and hopefully come to the conclusion that I was skilled enough. I submitted applications to four now-defunct studios: Radical Entertainment, Threewave, Blue Castle Games, and IUGO.

The application to Radical Entertainment went nowhere. Threewave had me fill out a questionnaire and did schedule a phone screen afterwards, but ultimately it didn't move forward either.

Blue Castle Games had me come in for an interview. They were hiring a junior network programmer for Dead Rising 2. Network programming for twitchy games was something I often thought about, and more importantly Mad Kaz did have an unfinished multiplayer networking implementation that was stripped out at the last second.

The interview was a disaster. One of the interviewers had gone through Mad Kaz's code. "So, this virtual machine of yours, all it is is a for loop?" Thinking he was deliberately trying to get a reaction out of me, I responded with a flat "Yes, sure." Later, during a whiteboarding segment, I was talking out a problem on how to model zombie movements. When one of the interviewers jumped in with a question, without thinking I blurted, "Wait, shut up" as a new thought entered my head. At the end was my first experience with interview brainteasers. The final interviewer asked me the three-prisoners hat riddle. It wasn't something I had thought to prepare for and so I froze.

IUGO had me come in for an interview after a personal appeal. I showed up to the interview running into a college acquaintance, not knowing beforehand that he worked there. During the interview, one of the interviewers had me sit down with a stack of papers full of game coding and design questions. When I finished, he tossed them aside, saying something to the effect of "I don't need to look at it. I know you know your stuff."

At the end of the interview, I spoke with the CEO. He had either given me an offer or was on the verge of giving one. I ended up walking away. I don't recall exactly why. It may have been because I was actively interviewing at Blue Castle Games, or I may have been feeling too full of myself and gunning for a more senior role, or it may have been because the office felt uncomfortably bright. It was unusually sunny that day. IUGO's office space let a lot of that sun in. I had spent most of my time building Mad Kaz in my blacked-out bedroom.

In the end, I was getting dangerously low on funds and so I accepted a job at a company that made calendar software. The salary was around 15% lower than that of the job I resigned from to enter the competition. Weeks after starting, I revisited the MOTODEV Game Developer Challenge website. Mad Kaz hadn't won, nor was it one of the finalists.

Addendum: Engine postmortem

Although the story of Mad Kaz is now over, I'd like to take a moment to write about the part of Mad Kaz that I found the most interesting: Mad Kaz's engine. Mad Kaz's engine was the culmination of my experiences with gaming and coding, starting from early childhood experiments on the first family PC my father purchased in the early 1990s all the way up to the job I resigned from to enter the competition.

As a game, Mad Kaz failed. As a game engine, it succeeded at doing something few at the time had even attempted: It was a UGC platform for the resource-constrained mobile devices of that era. The individual components of the engine weren't all that unique or original. Doom had already popularized pack files, Quake had already popularized scripting, QuakeWorld had already popularized in-game level downloads, countless games performed dynamic audio mixing and graphical post-processing, etc. It was the collection of those individual components together under a single umbrella, running successfully on a resource-constrained Java device, that made for something special.

Many of the individual components within Mad Kaz were born out of my experiences at airG, a company that's still around: https://corp.airg.com. airG's R&D head at the time was forgiving enough to look past my attitude problem and let me work under him. He made several attempts at a scriptable platform for their mobile apps. Those scriptable platforms, along with Java's virtual machine and Quake's virtual machine, became the inspiration for Mad Kaz's virtual machine and assembler.

Mad Kaz's PNG editing module was inspired from overhearing a watercooler discussion at airG. A senior engineer mentioned how someone they knew wrote code to go in and edit a PNG's palette at runtime. After hearing that, I visited a now-defunct website known for documenting file formats, pulled out the specifications for PNG, and spent a few days at work trying to come up with interesting things to do with palette modifications.

Mad Kaz's image manipulation functions were inspired by a college classmate who also worked with me at airG. He was the same classmate that introduced me to NS-SHAFT and NS-TOWER. While sitting in a college computer lab one day, he had written code to resize an image using bilinear scaling. We didn't know that it was called bilinear scaling at the time, but his idea of pixel-to-pixel gradients was the seed for Mad Kaz's image rotation, scaling, and post-processing effects.

Mad Kaz's image manipulation functions required rational numbers, but most mobile phones at that time didn't include a hardware floating point unit. Somewhere along the way, I had learned about fixed-point math and lookup tables. I used fixed-point math to implement a progress bar in one of airG's apps, and I also used it to perform the image manipulation math in Mad Kaz.

The list goes on. It was all prior art that, at some point or another, I had experienced and internalized in some way: Coding timeline.
