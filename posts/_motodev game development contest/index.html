Things I've tried: Chasing the Indie Gamedev Dream

MONTH DAY, YEAR

In 2007, at the age of 22, I decided to resign from what was arguably my first real software engineering job to enter a game development competition. The MOTODEV Game Developer Challenge, put on by Motorola, was a competition for indie gamedevs to develop a game for the Motorola KRZR K1.

My entry to the competition was Mad Kaz, a vertical platformer that was backed by a custom game engine I had built specifically for the competition. Mad Kaz was the culmination of my experiences around code and games, from early childhood when my father came home with our first family PC all the way up to resigning from my job.

This post recounts the story of how Mad Kaz came to be. It's been nearly 2 decades and so my memories around Mad Kaz and the competition are fuzzy. But, thankfully I still have the old source control repositories for the game as well as my old Thunderbird email profile which help fill in the gaps from that time.

Entering the competition

The MOTODEV Game Developer Challenge was opened to contestants on February 21, 2007 and ran until August 10, 2007. Around mid-March, I registered an LLC along with a domain name for the competition. Shortly afterwards, I notified my employer at the time, airG, that I was parting ways. I didn't give a reason for why I was leaving, just that I was leaving. Giving the reason, I thought, may attract the ire of the company.

As my last few days of employment were closing in, I went to dinner with a few close college friends and colleagues. I let them know that the reason I was leaving was to enter the competition. The competition itself had no huge prize or payout. The prize was nothing more than a publishing contract with a mid-tier mobile game publisher and some free Motorola devices. But, prizes didn't matter to me so much as the competition itself. It was, I was hoping, the start of something bigger.

Between late March and early April was when I officially began work on my entry. My original idea was a top-down 2D MMO virtual world, similar to Habbo Hotel but for mobile phones. I had pitched a similar idea to one of airG's owners a while back, at a lunch he had taken me to as a reward for fixing a costly bug.

He didn't like the idea. At the time, airG made a popular social networking app, not games. Even if airG did make games, such a game would be costly to produce and the market for it would be a tiny fraction of mobile phone owners. The mobile phones of the era were severely constrained and mobile Internet connectivity wasn't ubiquitous. Only those with high-end phones with Internet access would be able to play. If you were privileged enough to meet the criteria, you would still have to contend with the fact that mobile Internet at the time was slow and expensive.

Building the engine: The MMO that never was

Even with those hurdles in front of me, I still wanted to try my luck. At the time, I had around $7,500 to my name and was still living in my parent's home, in the same bedroom I grew up in. Before I could do anything, I needed a KRZR K1 or similar device. I went to a local store selling imported handsets and picked up the closest device in-stock to the KRZR K1, the Motorola SLVR L7. Alongside that I purchased a Samsung SGH-D520, just so I could have another device to try playing the game on.

With the devices in hand, the next step was to prep my bedroom as a work area. With only 3-4 months to deliver, I needed to minimize distractions. I took my mother's thick faux-fur blanket and duct taped its edges to the walls bordering my 108sqft bedroom's only window, effectively blacking out the room. The only light available to me, I thought, should be the light from the monitor directly in front of me.

I started out by making a plan. I charted out tasks and timelines in a Gantt chart, mocked out a UI flow, and mocked out a demonstration map. The idea was to have the MMO be a platform more so than a game. Scripts and assets would flow directly from the Internet, driving the game's logic and gameplay. The ultimate goal was to have a massive explorable world where users themselves would grow out the content.

From a technology perspective, everything in the plan was attainable, but not by a single developer with 3-4 months of time. The timelines, which were already wishful thinking to begin with, showed that development would stretch far past the competition's deadline of early August. And so, the MMO idea was a non-starter.

What wasn't a non-starter were the components in that timeline. Many of them, I figured, would be needed regardless of game I eventually decided on making. Between April 3 and April 10, I implemented the font renderer. The font renderer solved the issue of inconsistent fonts. J2ME didn't render text using a universal font across all devices makes and models, and it was impossible to switch out from the phone's default font. The font renderer would work around this by manually drawing out character glyphs.

A few days later, between April 12 and April 14, I implemented a lightweight Huffman tree. Mobile Internet connectivity back then was billed by usage. Huffman compression could save the player from an expensive mobile Internet bill.

A few days after that, between April 14 and May 3, I implemented a barebones virtual machine and assembler. A virtual machine would separate the game's logic from it's executable, similar to how QuakeC separated Quake's engine from the seemingly endless number of player-created maps and mods.

Yet a few days after that, between May 4 and May 7, I implemented image transformation functions and real-time audio mixing. The image transformations would make the game's art assets more flexible. The audio mixer, which worked on the emulator but not on the actual device, would workaround the device limits on playing multiple audio file at the same time.

Between early to mid-May, around the time the image and audio components were implemented, was when I finally settled on the game I wanted to implement.

Cutting scope: Choosing Mad Kaz

I decided that the game I wanted to implement would be a re-imagining of NS-SHAFT and NS-TOWER blended into one. NS-SHAFT and NS-TOWER were introduced to me by a college classmate as a way of killing time while we were held up in the computer labs. The twist with this re-imagining was that it'd be a narrative experience with levels, where each level would be unique in terms of graphics, audio, and gameplay mechanics.

I came up with a set of levels that I wanted the game to have. The title character, Mad Kaz (that's me, I'm Kaz 🙂), would climb up from inside the Earth and eventually out into space, before encountering a boss battle and falling back down to Earth. I named it Mad Kaz: The Beginning.

Shortly after, I began looking for contract artists to help piece it all together. For music, I found a Norwegian chiptunes musician by the name of Xonic the Fox. Xonic, around 19 at the time, produced 3 MIDI tracks based on a small set of requirements to hit certain evocative themes. The closest surviving archive of Xonic's website from that time is from September 2007, roughly a month after the competition ended: Technomancer.

For concept art, I reached out to an old high school classmate, William, to create a few basic sketches. There were no requirements. I simply asked him to envision how things should look.

For pixel art, I found Mykola, a Ukrainian pixel artist who was around 22 at the time. Mykola agreed to help reimagine the concept art as pixel art. Some of the art he built for the game is still on his old PixelJoint profile here and here. The closest surviving archive of Mykola's website from that time is from July 2007, around the time pixel art was flowing into the game: Myk31.

Cutting scope, again: Spread too thin

Even with a plan and the extra help, it turned out that it was all still too ambitious. The roughly 3 remaining months weren't enough time to both complete the game's engine and build out that much game logic and art. Pixel art didn't start flowing in until mid-June. I hadn't begun coding up the majority of the game's levels, outside of a few practice levels, until mid-July. Before that, I was still primarily trying to put the pieces of the game's engine together.

In old emails from Mykola, I could see that I was cracking under the pressure. Keeping myself confined to a blacked out room wasn't doing wonders for my mental health. With no outside light seeping in, it was impossible to tell if it was day or night. I would sleep and wake at random times, sometimes opening the bedroom door to use the kitchen or washroom thinking it was daytime only to realize it was the dead of night.

To cope, I decided to cut scope again. I scrapped the original level design and instead restructured the game into zones. Each zone would have multiple levels that re-used assets and had similar game logic, similar to original Sonic The Hedgehog games.

There were 6 zones in total: Intro, Homework, Hippie, Time Trials, Giagantor, and Author. Zone 1, Intro Zone, acted as a tutorial. It got the player familiar with gameplay mechanics. Zone 6, Author Zone, was a single level that acted as a credit sequence. It displayed the names and photos of friends and those who worked on the game. In between were Homework Zone, Hippie Zone, Time Trials Zone, and Giagantor Zone. Each zone attempted to have a unique twist to it: Zone breakdown and analysis.

Chronologically, these zones were built out in a different order than they appear in the game.

  • July 13-14, zone 1, Intro Zone, was built.
  • July 16-19, zone 2, Homework Zone, was built.
  • July 19-23, zone 6, Author Zone, was built.
  • July 21-26, zones 3 to 5, Hippie Zone, Time Trials Zone, and Giagantor Zone, were built.

Homework Zone, the one chronologically sandwiched between Intro Zone and Author Zone, was the zone closest to Mad Kaz's original story arc. It had cityscape climbing, racing downward to outrun a bomb, and there was even a boss fight. I had built it because I wanted to get a zone in where I didn't have to go back and forth with my pixel artist, Mykola, and so I had sat down and fleshed out some poorly drawn schoolbook-doodle type graphics in Microsoft Paint: Graph paper, pencil drawings, stick men, etc. The remaining zones 3-5 were added afterwards by gluing together various pixel art pieces into something that bordered on being passable.

I fleshed out these zones for as long as I could hold out. July 26th was when the last zone was completed. Bugfixes and tweaks ran through July 27th and 28th. There wasn't enough left in me to do much else after that. Even though there was still roughly 2 weeks remaining until the competition deadline, I was burnt out. I could have used that time. I never sat through a full playthrough of the entire game, or even a full playthrough of certain zones within the game. I could have balanced the gameplay to make the game actually fun to play (it wasn't). I could have fixed typos. I could have finished implementing the game engine's networking component. The list goes on ...

And so, in the end, Mad Kaz wasn't so much a game as much as it was a tech demo for a game engine. The 4 months I had spent putting things together weren't enough for me to build out both a game engine and the game's originally intended levels, and so the result was a mishmash of heavily unbalanced and narratively disjointed levels that tried to shove in as many engine features as possible.

Submission: The anticlimax

I submitted Mad Kaz into the competition on July 29th, 2007, about 2 weeks before the August 10th deadline. A confirmation email arrived the next day, and that was it. I never heard back from the people running the contest about their thoughts on Mad Kaz or how it ranked against other games in the competition. I showed the game to a couple of my real life friends and they thought it was alright. I had an online friend load it up on his phone and give it a try. He thought it was alright. I posted some screenshots in an IRC chatroom, to which someone replied saying the graphics were bad. I emailed my father informing him of what I was up to after 4 months unemployed, to which he replied with the common encouraging words that parents are known to say to their children. And, that was it.

Looking back, I wish there had been something more to make it more triumphant or satisfying in the end. I recall feeling dejected immediately after sending in the submission. Maybe it was because my entry wasn't as polished as what I was hoping for, or maybe it was from putting myself through 4 months of self-imposed abuse and verging on being broke.

Aftermath: Back to reality

Around that time, I was using a spreadsheet to keep track of my checking and savings balance. Money was beginning to run tight. The records show that I started the competition with around $7,500 to my name. By the time I submitted Mad Kaz in late July, I had around $1,500. A month later, it went down to $1,100. A month after that, it went further down to $600.

In early August, shortly after the submission was in, I began looking for work again. I sent applications to whatever places were looking to hire, but I was also trying to leverage my work on Mad Kaz to get hired at a game studio. I had decided to open-source the game's code and assets so potential interviewers could review what I did and hopefully come to the conclusion that I was skilled enough. I had submitted applications to Radical Entertainment, Threewave, Blue Castle Games, and a mobile game developer named IUGO. None of these studios are around anymore.

The application to Radical Entertainment went nowhere. Threewave had me fill out a questionnaire and did schedule a phone screen afterwards, but ultimately it didn't move forward either. I knew Threewave from their work on the Threewave CTF mod for Quake 1, which I regularly played growing up.

Blue Castle Games had me come in for an interview. They were hiring for a junior network programmer position for Dead Rising 2. I might have felt that I was a shoo-in for this position. Network programming for twitchy games was something I often thought about, mostly because of stories I heard about QuakeWorld's networking code and how it handled packet unreliability, latency, dead reckoning (extrapolating movements based on trajectory and latency), etc. And, more importantly, Mad Kaz did have an unfinished networking implementation that was stripped out at the last second prior to submission.

The interview was a disaster. It was the first time I was asked to sign an NDA prior to an interview. It was also the first time I was subjected to the typical software engineering interview hazing rituals that started becoming common around the mid-to-late 2000s. One of the interviewers had gone through Mad Kaz's code and decided to gauge my composure by making a snide comment. "So, this virtual machine of yours, all it is is a for loop?" I didn't give him the reaction that he wanted. During a whiteboarding segment, I was talking out a problem they gave me about how to model zombie movements. When one of them jumped in with a question, without thinking I blurted, "Wait, shut up" as a new thought entered my head. At the end was my first experience with interview brainteasers. The final interviewer asked me the three-prisoners hat riddle. I didn't even attempt an answer.

IUGO also had me come in for an interview. The owner and employees were very kind to me. I remember one of my old college classmates was working there as well. They had me sit down and fill out a stack of papers full of gaming coding and design questions. When I was done, the interviewer just threw it to the side, saying something to the effect of "I don't need to look at it. I know you know your stuff."

At the end of the interview, I think they gave me an offer or if they didn't they were on the verge of giving me an offer. I ended up walking away. I don't recall exactly why. It might have been because the interview at Blue Castle Games was coming up the next day, or I may have been feeling too full of myself and gunning for a more senior role, or it may have been because the office felt uncomfortably bright. It was a very sunny day that day. IUGO's office space let a lot of that sun in. I had spent most of my time building Mad Kaz in a blacked out room.

In the end, I was hurting for money and so I accepted a job at a company that made software to sync calendars between Google Calendar and Outlook. The salary was around 15% lower than that of the original job that I had resigned from.

Looking back

Mad Kaz was the collective, blended result of my experiences around gaming and coding, starting from early childhood, throughout highschool college, all the way up to the job I ultimately resigned from to enter the competition. As a game, it was objectively a failure. As a game engine, it succeeded at doing something few at the time had attempted: a UGC platform for the resource-constrained mobile devices of that era. The individual components weren't all that unique or original: Doom had already popularized pack files, Quake had already popularized scripting, QuakeWorld had already popularized in-game level downloads, countless games performed dynamic audio mixing and graphical post-processing effects, etc. It was the collection of those individual components together under a single umbrella, running successfully on a resource-constrained Java device, that made for something special.

Many of the individual components within Mad Kaz were born out of my experiences at airG, a company that's still around: https://corp.airg.com. airG's R&D head was forgiving enough to look past my attitude problem and let me work under him. He made several attempts at a scriptable platform for their mobile apps. Those scriptable platforms, along with Java's virtual machine and Quake's virtual machine, became the inspiration for Mad Kaz's virtual machine and assembler.

Mad Kaz's PNG editing module was inspired from overhearing a watercooler discussion at airG. A senior engineer mentioned how someone they knew wrote code to go in and edit a PNG's palette at runtime. After hearing that, I visited a now defunct website known for documenting file formats, pulled out the specifications for PNG, and spent a few days at work trying to come up with interesting things to do with palette modifications.

Mad Kaz's image manipulation functions were inspired by a college classmate who also worked with me at airG. He was the same one that introduced me to NS-SHAFT and NS-TOWER. To kill time while waiting in a computer lab, he had written code to resize an image using bilinear scaling. We didn't know that it was called bilinear scaling at the time, but his work was the seed for Mad Kaz's image rotation, scaling, and post-processing effects.

Mad Kaz's image manipulation functions required rational numbers, but most mobile phones at that time didn't include a floating point unit. Somewhere along the way, I had learned about fixed point math and lookup tables. I used fixed point math to implement a progress bar in one of airG's apps, and I also used it to perform the image manipulation math in Mad Kaz.

The list goes on. It was all prior art that, at some point or another, I had experienced and internalized in some way.
