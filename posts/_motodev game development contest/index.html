Things I've tried: Chasing the Indie Gamedev Dream

MONTH DAY, YEAR

In 2007, at the age of 22, I had decided to resign from what was arguably my first real software engineering job to enter a game development competition. The MOTODEV Game Developer Challenge, put on by Motorola, was a competition for indie gamedevs. The goal of the competition was to develop a game for one of two flagship Motorola phones that were out at the time, either the Motorola KRZR K1 or the Motorola Q line of phones.

My entry to the competition was Mad Kaz, a vertical platformer for the KRZR that was backed by a custom game engine I had built specifically for the competition. Mad Kaz was the culmination of my experiences around code and games, from early childhood when my father came home with our first family PC all the way up to resigning from my job. It's been nearly 2 decades and so my memories around Mad Kaz and the competition are fuzzy at best, but thankfully I still have the old source control repositories for the game as well as my old Thunderbird email profile which help fill in the gaps from that time.

Original entry description:

Mad Kaz is a vertical platformer where you lead the title character through a series of unique levels, each crazier than the last. Mad Kaz features six zones with between four to ten levels per zone, and eight different game modes. Built on top of a unique engine, Mad Kaz allows users to create their own levels with customized game play, cinematics, music, and style. Levels can be uploaded onto phones via either Bluetooth or the Internet. The source code packages for the engine, levels, and SDK can be found on the CodeYield Development site.

Entering the competition

The MOTODEV Game Developer Challenge was opened to contestants on Feb 21 and ran until Aug 10, 2007. Around mid-March, I had registered an LLC along with a domain name for the competition. Shortly afterwards, I notified my employer at the time, airG, that I was parting ways. I hadn't given an exact reason for why I was leaving, just that I was leaving. Part of the reason may have had to do with what I witnessed when a former colleague, after having a baby, attempted to leave for greener pastures to another mobile developer. They had prevented him from starting his new job over worries that he would take over trade secrets or other intellectual property.

As my last few days of employment were closing in, I recall being excited about the competition. I went to dinner with a few close college friends and colleagues, secretly letting them know that the reason I was leaving was to enter the competition. The competition itself had no huge prize or payout. The prize was nothing more than a publishing contract with a mid-tier mobile game publisher and some free Motorola devices. But, the prizes didn't matter to me so much as the competition itself. It was, I was hoping, the start of something bigger.

Between late March and early April is when I officially began work on my entry. My original idea was a 2D MMO virtual world, similar to Habbo Hotel but for mobile phones. I had pitched a similar idea to one of airG's directors a while back while still employed there. I had fixed a costly bug on a flagship phone that was preventing airG's app from shipping on the device, and so as a reward he had taken me to a lunch.

He wasn't entirely enthused by the idea. While he hadn't fully articulated the reasons, there were likely a few. At the time, airG made a popular social networking app, not games. Even if airG did make games, such a game would be resource-intensive to make and challenging to become a commercial success. Even then, such a game wouldn't be able to run on most devices, only the higher-end ones. The mobile phones of the era were severely resource-constrained. Devices often only had hundreds of kilobytes of heap, 1 to 10 megabytes of persistent storage, and screen dimensions between 100 and 200 pixels. Internet connectivity was also a problem. It was slow, inconsistent, heavily metered, and was billed based on usage. You didn't have to do a lot to get a big Internet usage bill.

Building the engine: The MMO that never was

Even with all of those hurdles, I still wanted to try. At the time, I had around $7,500 to my name, still living in my childhood home in the same bedroom I grew up in. Before I could do anything, I needed a KRZR K1 or similar device. I went to a local store selling imported handsets and picked up the closest device in stock to the KRZR K1, which ended up being the Motorola SLVR L7, along with a Samsung SGH-D520 just so I had another device I could try playing the game on. I then decided to black out my bedroom windows with a thick blanket and duct tape to prevent being distracted by outside light seeping in.

I started by making a plan. I charted out tasks and estimates in a Gantt chart, mocked out a UI flow, and mocked out a simple map that would be the base of the MMO. The idea was to have it be a platform more so than a game. Scripts would drive logic, where those scripts and related assets would flow in directly from the Internet. The ultimate goal was to have users generate their own content that made up a massive explorable world.

From a technology perspective, everything in that Gantt chart and those mockups was attainable, but not for a single developer with 3-4 months of time. The timelines within the chart, which were already wishful thinking to begin with, showed that development would stretch past the competition's deadline of early August. As such, the MMO wasn't possible. Nevertheless, there were components in the Gantt chart that I figured would be needed regardless of what game I ended up making, and so I began working on them while I thought of alternative game ideas.

Between April 3 and April 10, I implemented the font engine. Java-enabled phones of that era didn't normalize to a single specific font for all applications to use. Instead, each class of device had its own font and it was impossible to switch out to a different one. For example, the exact same app running on a Nokia S60 vs a Motorola KRZR would look different, because, among other reasons, the font used to render out a string of text was different. Most developers would work around this problem by using bitmap fonts, an image consisting of a grid of characters. I chose a different approach for my font engine. Somewhere along my engineering journey, I came across Hershey vector fonts, which were simple fonts defined as a chain of connected lines. They were free, easy to decode and render, and easy to apply transformations to such as rotating and scaling.

Between April 12 and April 14, I implemented a lightweight Huffman tree. A Huffman tree implementation wasn't part of the Gantt chart, but I figured that there would be some networking aspect to the game and so I wanted my network traffic to be compressed to keep things efficient. Less data being sent over the Internet meant there was less potential of chewing up the player's mobile Internet quota (and hopefully less likely that they'd end up with a large bill). There were no built-in compression packages in Java phones of that era and simple Java open-source implementations intended for resource-constrained devices weren't all that prevalent. Huffman was more or less the most advanced compression algorithm that I'd internalized and was comfortable with.

Between April 14 and May 3, I implemented the virtual machine and assembler. The intention with the virtual machine was to drive the game's logic and animations, similar to how the original Quake engine had a virtual machine that enabled Quake's gameplay as well as its near endless modifications. The virtual machine and assembler were both very basic. The virtual machine had opcodes similar to that of Java's virtual machine, and the assembler's syntax was similar to assemblers I had come across in my teens.

Between May 4 and May 7, I implemented audio and image functions. The functions, I had figured, would be helpful for whatever game I ended up with. There was a software audio mixer I wrote that turned out to be a failure. While the emulator would successfully mix the audio sources, the device itself wouldn't. There was also an image transformation suite which contained common manipulations such as scaling, rotation, colorization, brightness, and contrast.

Cutting scope: Choosing Mad Kaz

Sometime between early to mid-May, I decided that the game I wanted to implement would be a re-imagining of NS-SHAFT and NS-TOWER blended into one. NS-SHAFT and NS-TOWER were introduced to me by a college classmate as a way of killing time while we were held up in the computer labs. The twist with this re-imagining was that it'd be a narrative experience with levels, where each level would be unique in terms of graphics, audio, and gameplay mechanics.

I came up with a set of levels that I wanted the game to have. The title character, Mad Kaz (that's me, I'm Kaz 🙂), would climb up from inside the Earth and eventually out into space, before encountering a boss battle and falling back down to Earth. I named it Mad Kaz: The Beginning.

Cutting scope, again: Spread too thin

Even with a concrete plan and the extra help, it turned out that it was still too ambitious. The roughly 3 remaining months weren't enough time to both complete the game's engine and build out that much game logic and art. Pixel art didn't start flowing in until mid-June. I hadn't begun coding up the majority of the game's levels, outside of a few practice levels, until mid-July. Before that, I was still primarily trying to put the pieces of the game's engine together.

In old emails from Mykola, I could see that I was cracking under the pressure. I ended up deciding to scrap the original level design and instead restructure into zones. Each zone would have multiple levels that re-used assets and had similar game logic, similar to original Sonic The Hedgehog games.

There were 6 zones in total: Intro, Homework, Hippie, Time Trials, Giagantor, and Author. Zone 1, Intro Zone, acted as a tutorial. It got the player familiar with gameplay mechanics. Zone 6, Author Zone, was a single level that acted as a credit sequence. It displayed the names and photos of friends and those who worked on the game. AI analysis and breakdown of zones

Mad Kaz shipped with six zones. The zones are more than graphical themes: each one takes the same basic vertical-platformer machinery and explores a different idea.

Intro = control, Homework = block physics, Hippie = perception, Time Trials = indirect control, Giagantor = impact and environmental feedback, and Author = credits implemented as a level.

Intro

The Intro zone is a deliberately constructed tutorial rather than a collection of generic easy stages.

Level What it does Purpose
Free Drop Fall to the bottom with normal left/right steering. Introduces falling and horizontal control.
Free Climb Climb upward, charge jumps with 7, and steer or charge while airborne. Introduces the normal climbing and jump-charging model.
Limited Climb Removes horizontal control and airborne charging. Forces the player to commit to trajectories before jumping.
Limited Climb 2 Retains the movement restrictions and adds failure for falling out of view. Makes the scrolling camera itself part of the challenge.
Hard Drop Returns to descent with sparse platforms and failure for falling behind. Tests the movement system under pressure.

The progression is systematic: teach the unrestricted movement model, remove freedoms one at a time, then test the player under pressure. The abstract grid artwork also makes the zone look more like a training environment than a story location.

Homework

Homework is where the game starts changing what platforms mean, rather than simply changing where they are placed.

Level What it does Purpose
Homework Intro Introduces three platform types with different jump strengths, roughly 0x90, 0x110, and 0x7f. Asks the player to experiment and learn the new platform vocabulary.
Sunset City Reuses the same platform types under a roughly 50-second deadline. Places the tutorial mechanics inside an actual scenario.
Bomby Sunset 1 A long descent while a bomb is falling toward the city. Hitting platforms while moving quickly only reduces downward speed. Turns platform collisions into something the player has to manage during a rapid descent.
Bomby Sunset 2 Platforms remove much more downward velocity. Increases the penalty for poor collision choices.
Bomby Sunset 3 Platforms stop the player's descent completely. Completes the progression from mild slowdown to complete obstruction.
The Great Escape A giant stickman advances toward the player. Landing on the upper platform fires a math wave, with its strength based on falling velocity. The player must survive for 40 seconds. Turns an existing movement property into a combat mechanic.

The Great Escape is particularly revealing. The game does not add a conventional attack button. Instead, something the player already understands—falling velocity—becomes attack strength. Homework moves from learning unusual platform properties to deliberately exploiting physics.

Hippie

Hippie is fundamentally a perception-manipulation zone. The farther the player progresses, the less reliable the screen becomes.

Level What it does Purpose
Psychedelic Continuously changes the background through randomized colors. Introduces visual interference without greatly changing the mechanics.
Confusion 1 Standing on a colored platform changes the background to the same color. Begins making foreground and background difficult to separate.
Confusion 2 Uses four colors. Reduces the visual distinctions available to the player.
Confusion 3 Uses three colors. Further reduces foreground/background separation.
Confusion 4 Uses two colors. Makes the visual ambiguity stronger.
Confusion 5 Keeps the two-color setup and adds immediate failure for falling. Adds mechanical punishment to the visual confusion.
Flashbacks Loads imagery from the previous Homework zone, including graph-paper, boss, and wave elements. Makes the supposed mental flashbacks literal callbacks to earlier gameplay.
Checkers Uses a high-contrast checkerboard environment. Interferes with spatial perception through pattern rather than color alone.
Day Dreams Generates two different platform maps and swaps between them every five seconds while also manipulating color. Makes the level geometry itself unreliable.

The zone progresses from visual noise → foreground/background confusion → memories of earlier gameplay → actual instability of the level geometry.

Time Trials

The five stages are mechanically very similar, but increase sharply in scale: 200 → 400 → 800 → 1600 → 2000 floors, all with a target time of 2:56.

Level Distance What changes
200 Floor Sprint 200 floors Introduces the Time Trials rule set.
400 Floor Sprint 400 floors Same rules, twice the distance.
800 Floor Sprint 800 floors Same rules over a longer run.
1600 Floor Sprint 1600 floors Turns the mechanic into an endurance challenge.
2000 Floor Sprint 2000 floors The longest version of the same challenge.

Direct left/right control is disabled. Different platforms alter horizontal velocity, direction, or jump power, and normal horizontal movement is restored after leaving them. In other words, the platforms substitute for the steering controls. The player navigates by choosing which movement behavior the next platform should provide.

The zone is therefore about route planning under indirect control, followed by escalating endurance. It also shows how the scripting system could make the same character controller behave very differently without changing the Java engine.

The text for the 800 Floor Sprint accidentally calls it a "600 floor sprint"; the level data itself specifies 800.

Giagantor

Giagantor has the strongest connection between story and mechanics. Mad Kaz has been turned into a giant, so the levels revolve around his weight disturbing the environment.

Level What it does Purpose
Tippy Toe 1 Descend 500 floors. Landing too hard causes failure. Landing velocity also drives screen shake and phone vibration. Turns the player's own mass into the main hazard.
Tippy Toe 2 Same rule, but the platforms are farther apart. Makes soft landings harder to achieve.
Tippy Toe 3 Platforms are farther apart again. Further increases unavoidable falling speed.
Inner Tippy Toe 1 Platforms are obscured behind a building facade. The player is told to use the shaking caused by landing to locate hidden blocks. Changes screen shake from feedback into information.
Inner Tippy Toe 2 Uses the same idea with sparser platforms. Makes the hidden-platform problem harder.
Climb to Space 1 A 900-floor ascent while authorities fire bright beams from the sides. Screen shake warns that the beams are about to converge. Four hits are allowed. Changes shaking from information about the player's impact into warning of an outside threat.
Climb to Space 2 Beams converge faster and only three hits are allowed. Increases pressure while keeping the same mechanic.
Climb to Space 3 Beams converge much faster and a single hit ends the level. The strictest version of the beam-avoidance mechanic.

"Don't attract attention" is represented through actual impact velocity, rather than through a separate stealth meter. Later, the same environmental effect is reused in different ways.

The zone has a clear progression: your giant size makes your own movement dangerous → screen shaking becomes useful information → the same shaking becomes a warning of an external attack.

The beam attack is also implemented partly by moving the permissible side boundaries inward, another example of the presentation being built through changes to the underlying game rules rather than through a separate enemy system.

Author Zone

Level What it does Purpose
Authors and Friends Cycles through photographs of Kasra, Mykola, Xonic, Farzad/Farshad, James "Ghaleon," and Ed "Eduardo," changing the image every 15 seconds. Implements the game's credits as an ordinary scripted level.

There is also a very 2007-J2ME technical detail here: after switching photographs, the script explicitly invokes garbage collection, presumably because keeping several full-screen images around on the target heap was undesirable.

The entire credits sequence is therefore another example of the VM's flexibility: rather than creating a dedicated credits subsystem, the game implements the credits using the same programmable level system as everything else.

Chronologically, these zones were built out in a different order than they appear in the game.

  • July 13-14, zone 1, Intro Zone, was built.
  • July 16-19, zone 2, Homework Zone, was built.
  • July 19-23, zone 6, Author Zone, was built.
  • July 21-26, zones 3 to 5, Hippie Zone, Time Trials Zone, and Giagantor Zone, were built.

Homework Zone, the one chronologically sandwiched between Intro Zone and Author Zone, was the zone closest to the original story. It had a cityscape, climbing up, racing down attempting to outrun a bomb, and there was even a boss fight. The remaining zones 3-5 were likely added afterwards as disjointed filler content, because Homework Zone by itself basically wasn't anywhere near good enough to be the entire game.

While Homework Zone had a rustic, scrappy charm to it, it didn't look or play great. I had built it because I wanted to get a zone in where I didn't have to go back and forth with my pixel artist, Mykola, and so I had sat down and fleshed out some poorly drawn schoolbook-doodle type graphics in Microsoft Paint. Graph paper, stick men, pencil drawings, etc...

I was fleshing out these 6 zones up until the very end. July 26th was when the last zone was completed, with bugfixes and tweaks running through July 27th and 28th. The entire game was submitted into the competition on July 29th. There wasn't enough time to balance the gameplay. Even if there was, I don't think I had it in me to know what fair balancing would entail or feel like.

And so, in the end, Mad Kaz wasn't so much a narrative experience that progressively took advantage of the engine's features as much as it was a tech demo for an engine that I had built in those 4 months. Those 4 months weren't enough time for me to build out both the engine and the originally intended levels, and so the game ended up simply being an unbalanced mess that tried to shove in as many engine features as possible.

Submission: The anticlimax

I submitted Mad Kaz into the competition on July 29th, 2007, about 2 weeks before the August 10th deadline. A confirmation email arrived the next day, and that was it. I never heard back from the people running the contest about their thoughts on Mad Kaz or how it ranked against other games in the competition. I showed the game to a couple of my real life friends and they thought it was alright. I had an online friend load it up on his phone and give it a try. He thought it was alright. I posted some screenshots in an IRC chatroom, to which someone replied saying the graphics were bad. I emailed my father informing him of what I was up to after 4 months unemployed, to which he replied with the common encouraging words that parents are known to say to their children. And that was it.

Aftermath: Back to reality

Around that time, I was using a spreadsheet to keep track of my checking and savings balance. Money was beginning to run tight. The records show that I started the competition with around $7,500 to my name. By the time I submitted Mad Kaz in late July, I had around $1,500. A month later, it went down to $1,100. A month after that, it went further down to $600.

In early August, shortly after the submission was in, I began looking for work again. I sent applications to whatever places were looking to hire, but I was also trying to leverage my work on Mad Kaz to get hired at a game studio. I had decided to open-source the game's code and assets so potential interviewers could review what I did and hopefully come to the conclusion that I'm skilled enough. I had submitted applications to Radical Entertainment, Threewave, Blue Castle Games, and a mobile game developer named IUGO. None of these studios are around anymore.

The application to Radical Entertainment went nowhere. Threewave had me fill out a questionnaire and did schedule a phone screen afterwards, but ultimately it didn't move forward either. I knew Threewave from their work on the Threewave CTF mod for Quake 1, which I regularly played growing up.

Blue Castle Games had me come in for an interview. They were hiring for a junior network programmer position for Dead Rising 2. I might have felt that I was a shoo-in for this position. Network programming for twitchy games was something I often thought about, mostly because of stories I heard about QuakeWorld's networking code and how it handled packet unreliability, latency, dead reckoning (extrapolating movements based on trajectory and latency), etc. And, more importantly, Mad Kaz did have an unfinished networking implementation that was stripped out at the last second prior to submission.

The interview was a disaster. It was the first time I was asked to sign an NDA prior to an interview. It was also the first time I was subjected to the typical software engineering interview hazing rituals that started becoming common around the mid-to-late 2000s. One of the interviewers had gone through Mad Kaz's code and decided to deliberately provoke as a means to gauge my composure. "So, this virtual machine of yours, all it is is a for loop?" I didn't give him the reaction that he wanted. During a whiteboarding segment, I was talking out a problem they gave me about how to model zombie movements. When one of them jumped in with a question, without thinking I blurted "wait, shut up" as a new thought entered my head. At the end was my first experience with interview brainteasers. The final interviewer asked me the three-prisoners hat riddle. I didn't even attempt an answer.

IUGO also had me come in for an interview. The owner and employees were very kind to me. I remember one of my old college classmates was working there as well. They had me sit down and fill out a stack of papers full of gaming coding and design questions. When I was done, the interviewer just threw it to the side, saying something to the effect of "I don't need to look at it. I know you know your stuff."

At the end of the interview, I think they gave me an offer or if they didn't they were on the verge of giving me an offer. I ended up walking away. I don't recall exactly why. It might have been because the interview at Blue Castle Games was coming up the next day, or I may have been feeling too full of myself and gunning for a more senior role, or it may have been because the office felt uncomfortably bright. It was a very sunny day that day. IUGO's office space let a lot of that sun in. I had spent most of my time building Mad Kaz in a blacked out room.

In the end, I needed money and so I accepted a job at a company that made software to sync calendars between Google Calendar and Outlook. The salary was noticeably lower than that of the original job that I had resigned from.

Looking back

Looking back, this story's archetype wasn't uncommon for the mid-to-late 2000s era of computing. A young individual, inexperienced but full of themself, and having grown up with video games of the 1990s and early 2000s, saves up just enough money to leave whatever it is they're doing to gamble on making their own indie game. Their hope is that what ends up coming out is both worthwhile and accepted by the market, similar to Commander Keen.

That hope rarely becomes reality. Instead, reality becomes a set of humbling experiences. Dreaming up an idea only to have to continually water it down at each stage, hoping people you're relying on will be able to follow through in time, having to explain to loved ones why you willingly left behind gainful employment, watching what little money you have evaporate, and ultimately starting with hope and ending with the bittersweet feeling of completing what you set out to do only to crawl back to the grind.

In the end, I'm grateful that I actually managed to produce something to submit for the competition, even if it was a far cry from what I originally wanted.

Addendum: How the technology came to be

When I ask AI to do a critical, in-depth analysis of Mad Kaz and compare against the closest known contemporaries of its time, here's what it says:

As executable software, its value is low. The original services are not demonstrably operational today, the MIDP/JSR-82 deployment environment is obsolete, and the supplied source/build infrastructure requires reconstruction. The old project endpoints were not accessible through my current web checks.

As technical prior art, its value is much higher. It documents a complete pre-smartphone attempt at programmable mobile UGC before EA was publicly marketing phone-created/shared levels in Boom Blox in 2008. That does not prove influence, but it is historically significant chronology.

As an educational artifact, it remains unusually rich: constrained bytecode-interpreter design, compiler/runtime agreement, custom packfiles, capability narrowing, J2ME rendering, RMS persistence, HTTP retrieval and Bluetooth protocols all exist together in source.

Its most useful lesson today may be the contrast inside the same artifact: very strong systems ambition and product integration can coexist with weak sandbox resource governance, weak reproducibility, poor author tooling and almost nonexistent verification evidence. The project was therefore not “mediocre.” It was exceptionally ambitious and probably highly original at its actual product layer, surprisingly complete, technically sophisticated, and simultaneously far too brittle to deserve a strong production-engineering rating.

Nothing in Mad Kaz was an original idea. But, the collection of all of its constituent pieces together made for something that no one had attempted before on mobile phones. It was the collective, blended result of my experiences around gaming and coding, starting from my childhood, to my time at college, all the way up to the job working on mobile phone apps that I ultimately resigned from to enter the competition.

The idea for a virtual machine came from the original Quake game as well as Java. I had grown up playing Quake, a game I was never very good at but always loved because of how QuakeC and the accompanying virtual machine enabled a near endless amount of mods and maps to be produced (Threewave CTF, Future vs Fantasy, Rocket Arena, AirQuake, Team Fortress, Quake Rally, and Cujo were some of the ones I remember). Similarly, in high school we had programming classes where we learned how to code in Java 1.2, and somewhere along the line I eventually became aware that Java's write-once run-anywhere claim was possible because there was a virtual machine and interpreter sitting in between.

The idea for Bluetooth transfers and Internet downloads of new levels, which was possible because of the virtual machine, was also heavily inspired by Quake and the myriad of later games that had similar technology. My earliest recollection was QuakeWorld, where a client would download a map from the server if it didn't already exist. The only thing that was different with Mad Kaz's implementation was that it was on a phone, and phones of this era were severely resource constrained.

The image manipulation package had modules for resizing, rotation, pixel post-processing, and editing PNGs. The ideas behind those modules came from a few different places. I likely was introduced to the concept of non-trivial resizing and rotation back in college. That same classmate who introduced me to NS-SHAFT and NS-TOWER had decided one day to kill time by writing code to resize an image using bilinear scaling. I don't recall if we knew that it was called bilinear scaling at the time. He was naturally gifted and it's entirely possible that he reasoned about the entire process on his own, but it was something that stuck with me as a core memory and likely was the seed for the image rotation and resizing in Mad Kaz.

Those image manipulation operations required rational numbers, but many phones at the time, including the Motorola KRZR, either didn't support floating point or did support it but it wasn't fast enough for heavy usage (e.g., emulated through software). Somewhere along the way, either at my employer or earlier, I had learned about fixed point math and lookup tables.
