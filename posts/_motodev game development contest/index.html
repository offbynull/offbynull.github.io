Things I've tried: Becoming an Indie Gamedev

November 12, 2026

In 2007, at the age of 22, I resigned from what was arguably my first real software engineering job to enter Motorola's MOTODEV Game Developer Challenge, an indie gamedev competition for the then-flagship KRZR K1. My entry to the competition was Mad Kaz, a vertical platformer backed by a custom game engine.

Mad Kaz was my first attempt at building a real, non-trivial game. I pieced it together in the span of four months, working in my 108sqft bedroom. Most of that time was spent building an elaborate game engine rather than the game itself, and so Mad Kaz the game was trash. The game engine, on the other hand, turned out pretty well. It was a programmable UGC platform built from scratch by a lone 22 year old engineer and successfully ran on a device with 800kb of heap. It had a virtual machine and assembler, font system, graphical post processing, downloadable levels, level sharing, windowing system, etc. Many of the engine features were inspired by the games I grew up playing in the 1990s and early 2000s.

Below is the playable game along with anecdotes and artifacts detailing the game's development and struggles. Use the "Next" and "Previous" buttons to flip forward and backward.

  • Mad Kaz code - The game's source code and game assets, as they were when the game was submitted to the competition.

Work area (1/10)

Back around the time of the competition, I was still living in my childhood home with my mother. My 108sqft bedroom was where I built Mad Kaz. I prepared it as a work area by duct taping my mother's thick faux-fur blanket to the walls bordering the room's only window. The intention was to minimize distractions by blacking out the room. There was a queen sized box-spring bed with its head pushed up against the wall with the window. Butted up against the middle of the wall to the window's right was an IKEA HEMNES desk with my PC and a single flatscreen monitor.

Except for food and washroom breaks, I spent four months locked in this room building Mad Kaz.

Test devices (2/10)

The competition launched in late February but I didn't enter it until early April. Given the time crunch, it didn't make sense to place an online order for an unlocked KRZR and sit around waiting for it to arrive. Instead, I went to a local store selling imported mobile phones and purchased the closest device I could find: The Motorola SLVR. The SLVR and the KRZR had similar features, but the SLVR was under-specced. My thinking at the time was that if the game ran fine on the SLVR, it would run fine on the KRZR.

Alongside the SLVR, I also purchased a Samsung SGH-D520, just so I could have another device to test with. The shop owner cut the tax off the price because I bought two phones and paid cash.

Origins of the engine (3/10)

Before coming up with Mad Kaz, I was planning to make an entirely different game. My original game idea was a social top-down 2D MMO with mini-games and an explorable world that players would expand with their own content. I created documents mocking out how it would look and how I would go about building it.

I ditched the MMO idea early on because, even if it all went according to plan, development would have stretched well past the competition's deadline of early August. What I didn't ditch were the game engine components highlighted in the work plan. I figured that many of them would still be needed regardless of the game idea I finally settled on.

Before ultimately deciding on Mad Kaz as the game to build, I spent a week building a font renderer, half a week on a Huffman compressor, three weeks on a virtual machine and assembler, and one week on image transformations and real-time audio mixing.

Origins of Mad Kaz (4/10)

Mad Kaz was intended to be a reimagining of the games NS-SHAFT and NS-TOWER, blended into a unified narrative experience with levels. Each level was to be unique in terms of graphics, audio, and gameplay mechanics. I came up with a story arc along with a list of levels. The title character, Mad Kaz, would climb up from inside the Earth and eventually out into space, before encountering an alien boss battle and falling back down to Earth.

Mad Kaz's art (5/10)

To help piece Mad Kaz's levels together, I contracted out to several artists. A chiptunes musician by the name of Xonic the Fox produced three MIDI tracks. A few basic concept art sketches were created by a high school classmate, William. Those sketches then went to Mykola, a pixel artist who agreed to do the important work of reimagining them as pixel art.

Scaling down Mad Kaz (6/10)

There wasn't enough time to both complete Mad Kaz's engine and build out Mad Kaz's original level plan. To try and make things work, I restructured Mad Kaz's level plan into six zones. Each zone was to have multiple levels that re-used assets and had similar game logic.

Of those six zones, two were writeoffs. The first zone was a tutorial and the last zone was nothing more than in-game credit sequence cycling through names and photos of friends and people who worked on the game. The four zones in the middle were the meat of the game.

Zone breakdown and analysis

Old emails from that time show that I wasn't keeping it together. I hadn't properly scoped out the game's development and dumped too much time into the engine's development. My blacked out bedroom also wasn't helping. It was impossible for me to tell if it was day or night. I was sleeping and waking at random times.

Mad Kaz submission (7/10)

July 26th was when the last zone was completed. Bugfixes and tweaks ran through July 27th and 28th. I submitted Mad Kaz to the competition on July 29th. There were still 2 weeks remaining until the competition's deadline, but money was running tight and I was burning out.

Mad Kaz's fundamental flaw was that it just wasn't fun to play. It ultimately ended up becoming a game engine tech demo rather than a game, consisting of a mishmash of unbalanced and narratively disjointed levels that tried to shove in as many engine features as possible. In a perfect world, I would have spent those remaining 2 weeks play testing and polishing the game play as much as possible.

I showed Mad Kaz to a few friends, both real-life and online. Many of them didn't understand or care about the technical merits of the engine. It simply came off as a middling game. I also wrote my father, living in California at the time, letting him know what I had been up to during the four months I'd been unemployed. I don't think he fully grasped what I had been up to. His response was that I would always be a winner to him.

Post-submission job hunt (8/10)

Around that time, I was using a spreadsheet to track my checking and savings balance. Money was beginning to run tight. The records show that I started the competition with around $7,500 to my name. By the time I submitted Mad Kaz in late July, I had around $2,500 remaining and was on a steep downward trajectory.

I began looking for work again almost immediately after submitting Mad Kaz. I applied anywhere that was hiring, but I was also trying to leverage Mad Kaz to get hired at a real game studio. I ended up open-sourcing Mad Kaz in the hopes that a technically-inclined reviewer would look through it and come to the conclusion that I was skilled enough. I sent applications to four now-defunct studios: Radical Entertainment, Threewave, Blue Castle Games, and IUGO.

The application to Radical Entertainment went nowhere. Threewave had me fill out a questionnaire and scheduled a phone screen afterwards, but ultimately it didn't move forward either.

Blue Castle Games had me come in for an interview. They were hiring a junior network programmer for Dead Rising 2. It didn't go well. An interviewer had gone through Mad Kaz's code and made derisive comments in what felt like an unsuccessful attempt to shit test me. Another interviewer had me whiteboarding how to model zombie movements. He jumped in with a question at the same time as a competing thought was entering my head and I inadvertently told him "Wait, shut up." The interview ended with a brain teaser that had me fumbling and freezing up (three-prisoners hat riddle).

IUGO had me come in for an interview after I made a personal appeal to the CEO. The interview experience was the complete opposite to that of the Blue Castle Games interview. Everything was relaxed. A college acquaintance was working there. During the interview, one of the interviewers had me sit down with a stack of papers full of game coding and design questions. When I finished, he tossed them aside, saying something to the effect of "I don't need to look at it. I know you know your stuff."

The last part of the interview was with the CEO. I don't recall the full details but he had either given me an offer or was on the verge of giving one. I ended up walking away. I don't recall exactly why. It may have been because I was actively interviewing at Blue Castle Games, or I may have been feeling too full of myself and gunning for a more senior role, or it may have been because the office felt uncomfortably bright. It was unusually sunny that day. IUGO's office space let a lot of that sun in. I had spent most of my time building Mad Kaz in my blacked out bedroom.

In the end, I was dangerously low on funds and so I accepted a job at a company that made calendar software. The salary was around 15% lower than that of the job I resigned from to enter the competition. Weeks after starting, I revisited the MOTODEV Game Developer Challenge website. Mad Kaz hadn't won, nor was it one of the finalists. I never heard from the people running the competition about their thoughts on Mad Kaz or how it ranked against other games in the competition.

Engine postmortem (9/10)

Mad Kaz's engine was the culmination of my experiences with gaming and coding, starting from early childhood experiments on the first family PC my father purchased in the early 1990s all the way up to the job I resigned from to enter the competition.

As a game, Mad Kaz failed. As a game engine, it succeeded at doing something few at the time had attempted: It was a programmable UGC platform for the resource-constrained mobile devices of that era. The individual components of the engine weren't all that unique or original. Doom had already popularized pack files, Quake had already popularized scripting, QuakeWorld had already popularized in-game level downloads, countless games performed dynamic audio mixing and graphical post-processing, etc. It was the collection of those individual components together under a single umbrella, running successfully on an Java device with 800kb heap, that made for something special.

Many of the individual components within Mad Kaz were born out of my experiences at my former employer, a company that's still around as of the time this was written. The R&D head at the time was forgiving enough to look past my attitude problem and let me work under him. He made several attempts at a scriptable platform for their mobile apps. Those scriptable platforms, along with Java's virtual machine and Quake's virtual machine, became the inspiration for Mad Kaz's virtual machine and assembler.

Mad Kaz's PNG editing module was inspired from overhearing a watercooler discussion one day at work. A senior engineer mentioned how someone they knew wrote code to go in and edit a PNG's palette at runtime. After hearing that, I visited a now-defunct website known for indexing file format specifications, pulled out the specifications for PNG, and spent a few days at work trying to come up with interesting things to do with palette modifications.

Mad Kaz's image manipulation functions were inspired by a college classmate. While sitting in a college computer lab one day, he wrote code to resize an image using bilinear scaling. We didn't know that it was called bilinear scaling at the time, but his idea of pixel-to-pixel gradients was the seed for Mad Kaz's image rotation, scaling, and post-processing effects.

Mad Kaz's image manipulation functions required rational numbers, but most mobile phones at that time didn't include a hardware floating point unit. Somewhere along the way, I had learned about fixed-point math and lookup tables. I used fixed-point math to implement a progress bar in one of my employer's mobile apps, and I also used it to perform the image manipulation math in Mad Kaz.

The list goes on. It was all prior art that, at some point or another, I had experienced and internalized in some way.

Incorporating (10/10)

One unimportant but mildly interesting fact about Mad Kaz and the competition was that I incorporated a company to enter the competition with: CodeYield Development Inc. I contracted out the company's website design to a high school friend and got myself a fax number. For some reason, I thought that people wouldn't take the company seriously unless it had a fax number.
